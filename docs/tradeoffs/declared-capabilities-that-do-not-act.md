# Declared capabilities that do not act

**Gain:** every binding that crosses an authoritative boundary already declares what it needs, and the surfaces that would honour those declarations are built. `workspace.write` and `workspace.replace` name a capability and the path it would touch, `edits.apply`, `processes.start`, and `mcp.call` do the same for their own inputs, and the transcript, the `AuthorizationCard` block, the renderer's `Authorization pending` row, its `[a] Approve   [d] Deny` affordance, and the gateway's `approveTurn`/`denyTurn` response path all exist. `processes.stop` likewise takes a process id and reports what it stopped. Connecting either is one seam rather than a redesign.

**Cost:** two of those declarations presently do nothing. Generalist resolves a declared capability through its `Approvals` service and approves immediately when no host supplies one, and Rika supplies none, so a cell writes a file, replaces a range, starts a process, or calls an MCP server without anyone being asked, and no authorization ever reaches the transcript — the approve and deny affordances cannot appear however the model behaves. Separately, `processes.start` and `processes.status` reach the process registry that the coding-tool runtime builds for itself, while `processes.stop` reads the registry the binding surface provides, and each build closes over its own id counter and its own entry map; so a cell can start a process and watch it, but stopping one always reports that no process is registered under that id, and a user who starts long work from a cell can only wait for it or cancel the whole turn. In both cases the declaration describes an intent rather than an authority, and reading the binding source overstates what the product does.

**Rejected:** removing the declarations would make the bindings honest but throw away the design and leave nothing to connect. Providing `Approvals` now would decide, in passing, which capabilities require consent and how a denial is reported — a policy about trust that belongs to the product rather than to whichever change happens to wire the service. Sharing one registry is the right repair for `stop`, but its finalizer terminates every process it tracks, so which scope owns it decides when a user's work is killed; choosing that at the end of a migration, against behaviour no test covers, risks a worse defect than the one it fixes. Completing them means providing `Approvals` from the composition root together with that policy, and giving the binding surface and the tool runtime one registry with a single owner for its finalizer. `app.tui.test.ts` "approves a pending write authorization from the transcript and denies the next one" already describes the finished approval behaviour and is kept failing rather than deleted, so that gap stays visible; nothing exercises `processes.stop` at all, which is why this one stayed hidden.
